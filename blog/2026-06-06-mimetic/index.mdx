I got into photography because I like how it forces me to look. Not just see, but really look at something. There is a focus that comes with trying to make a good photograph that is difficult to replicate any other way. It is, at its best, a practice of presence. However, when traveling to certain places, you're immediately made aware that this is not an entirely innocent hobby. In this blog post, I want to wrestle some of my thoughts around **mimetic desire**, **social media**, and **influencer culture**.

## Your Wish is My Desire

Many people who are interested in travel or landscape photography are used to doing a bit of research when going traveling. Either for finding good locations, ticking off some of the things you'd like to see, or learning about the places you didn't even know you wanted to see.  

I'm sure I'm not alone in saying that during this process, we've seen images someone else has taken and thought "*Ohh, I'd love to go there and get a photo like this*". At face value, it's a pretty benign wish... but on inspection, may contribute to larger problems.

1. Multiply that desire across millions of people becoming inspired by the same image to go to this place.
2. The photograph features a sunset and flowers that are only captured in summer. So, better be there with thousands of other people who also need to be there at the same time, as the same time of day.
3. ***You too*** could have this photograph and post it on instagram and ***you too***, will be a source of desire for someone else's journey.

The first two points lead to fairly obvious conclusions; a large amount of people collected in the same place. Overtourism is a big problem in certain places of the world and, in some cases, can even cripple infrastructure for locals. Solving this requires change from the local authorities which is often retroactive instead of proactive, and is hard for us as individuals to solve. 

The last point is the more philosophical one that leads back into points 1 and 2. Primarily, this speaks to ***your*** motivation and desire to be somewhere or have a particular photograph. This desire in your head was put there by someone or something else and now, you're chasing across the world for imagery that has been completely curated by influencers. Without thinking it, you are sold on the idea of what this performance offers. This mechanism has a name, and it existed long before instagram...

## Introducing Mimetic Desire

<div className="pullquote">

*Man is the creature who does not know what he wants [...] and he turns to others in order to make up his mind. We desire what others desire because we imitate their desires.*

<span className="pullquote-attr">— René Girard [@Girard1987]</span>

</div>

This cheery quote is from the American-French Philosopher, René Girard. He coined the term ***’Mimetic desire’***, arguing that desire is constantly mimetic, i.e., duplicated, propelled, created, proposed and generated by the other person’s desire [@Lebratron2019]. 

The structure of mimetic desire is triadic, involving three elements: a subject (you), an object (the identity, action, or outcome of desire), and the mediator or model, (the person whose desire you're imitating). In this triad, the mediator is the key. It's the element that gives the object it's value, making it tangible, desirable, and disseminating this desire to all the subjects who observe it.

In the context of this discussion, the **mediator** is the influencer: the content creator whose curated photo shows them standing in front of the white-washed walls of Santorini who is there to sell you the promise. The **object** is the constellation of entities that make this desirable. It can be different things to different people (carefree living, followers, likes, relaxation with a breathtaking view) but ultimately, it's *this* photograph that has sold you on this meaning. As the **subject**, you are not really responding to the place, you are responding to a model's performed relationship with that place [@Arzbaecher2022]. *Your desire* is to mimic the photograph you saw, because that *image itself* had desire. 

Social media is a feed of desire. It presents pre-packaged, pre-endorsed, pre-experienced imagery of mediators showcasing their performed ease. It doesn't matter that behind the camera is a long line of people queuing for 45 minutes to get the exact same photograph. The aesthetics of the image and their apparent freedom is the goal, the destination is just the occasion and the stage for the performance.

## The Hermeneutic Circle

There is a phrase borrowed from philosophy for the photograph's role in all of this. In its original form, the ***hermeneutic circle*** describes how our understanding is always in motion. I.e., as you move toward the horizon of something, the horizon itself adapts. 

Albers and James, wrote about this circularity in the context of travel photography [@Albers1988]. The circle, begins with the photographic appearances that advertise and anticipate a trip. It then moves through the search for those images in the actual experience of travel. It ends with the traveller certifying and sealing the very same images in their own photographic production. You see the image, you seek the image, you reproduce the image, and the circle closes. In doing so, affirming its value, and transforming a lived moment into an object, something you *own* rather than something you *inhabit*.

For social media, the experience isn't just about the affirmation of reproducing the photograph, but also the sense of 'confirmation' from posting it. The confirmation of the experience that you desired is *itself* the experience. Feedback and engagement is an extension of this experience that confirms the worth of everything that was originally sought through mimetic desire.

If you want a practical example of how images are replicated, the tongue-in-cheek account [**@insta_repeat**](https://www.instagram.com/insta_repeat) on Instagram illustrates the circulation of the same content. It posts grids of identical photographs from the same locations. The woman in the hat facing away from the camera... the mirrored lake reflection of a mountain... the lone figure in the vast landscape. You don't have to read the captions to get the point. The point being made is not that any individual photographer is doing something wrong, but that the aggregate produces a global machine for manufacturing the same image, over and over, in a place that must bear the weight of millions of people arriving to take that same photograph [@Arzbaecher2022]. Something, that has real practical implications for places that are not built to handle the influx of influencers when an image goes viral.

## OK, So What?

<div className="pullquote">

*What is sought for in a holiday is a set of photographic images which have already been seen*

<span className="pullquote-attr">— Urry & Larson (1992) [@Urry1992]</span>

</div>

In 2023, [**Santorini received 3.4 million tourists**](https://www.cnn.com/travel/santorini-greece-overtourism-cruise-visitor-cap), against a permanent population of around 15,000 people. On peak days, up to 17,000 cruise passengers disembark from a single docking. There are now more than 8 million posts tagged **#santorini** on Instagram. In a cruel twist, the island has become subordinate to its own image. Locals working in luxury tourism can profit greatly from this exposure and are likely very grateful, if not only for two months of the year. But what of the locals not in tourism? Venice receives over [**20 million tourists per year**](https://veniceinsiderguide.com/venice-population-vs-tourists/). Its resident population is down from around 175,000 in the 1950s to under 50,000 today as locals are priced out by short-term rental platforms and the economic pressure of a city functioning primarily as a backdrop. In Mallorca, which saw 18 million visitors against a resident population of 1.2 million, [**protesters took to the streets**](https://www.aljazeera.com/gallery/2024/7/22/thousands-protest-over-tourism-in-spains-mallorca) carrying banners that read *"This isn't tourismophobia, it's numbers: 1,232,014 residents, 18 million tourists"* and *"Your luxury, our misery."* 

In Ubud, Bali, the authentic market culture has been displaced by Instagram 'prop shops' selling flower crowns, aesthetic breakfast bowls, decorative items designed not for use, but for photography. Entire rice paddies have been [**converted into swing installations**](https://troora.com/culture/overtourism-in-bali-when-culture-becomes-content/), actual physical infrastructure built purely to generate content. Not extreme enough, how about physical infrastructure that everybody hates? On Nusa Penida, a single cliff-edge photograph of Kelingking Beach went viral on Instagram around 2018. The ridge is shaped like a T-Rex and the image was shared thousands of times with hordes of people arriving to replicate it. By 2023, a [**182-metre glass elevator was under construction**](https://www.expatgo.com/2025/11/04/the-cliffside-lift-that-instagram-built-and-now-detests/) to carry the volume of tourists down to the beach. It was ordered for demolition in 2025 after widespread outrage (much of it from social media users). The platform that made the place famous had built the thing it now resented. 

During the 2026 winter olympics in the Dolomites, a photogenic church in the Funes Valley became a popular 'object' for influencer collection. What was once a pay-off for a long hike, is now a [**50~km round trip taxi ride drive up destination just to snap a selfie**](https://www.reuters.com/business/environment/no-selfies-please-dolomites-push-back-against-overtourism-ahead-games-2026-01-15/). In Fujikawaguchiko, Japan, officials [**erected a barrier blocking**](https://edition.cnn.com/travel/lawsons-fujikawaguchiko-japan-overtourism-intl-hnk) a popular Mount Fuji photo spot after visitors climbed rooftops and ignored safety rules. The city later [**cancelled its annual sakura celebration**](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c1wzrlndzjro). The event typically draws in 200,000 visitors, but after residents reported littering, trespassing, and tourists wandering into private homes, it had to be sadly cancelled. 

This collection and confirmation attitude that has been exacerbated by social media and mimetic desire either ends up destroying the place, or requires communities pushing back insisting that the place is not merely an image, and that the people who live there are not props in someone else's content.


## One 'McMindfulness' Takeaway, Please

Overtourism is the most visible consequence of the mimetic loop, but the same engine runs elsewhere. The wellness industry offers a subtler but structurally identical version of the same dynamic. During the last decade, and particularly during Covid, "Mindfulness" has had a considerable boost in popularity. 

Forest bathing retreats, silent meditation journeys, digital detox packages, and Luxury wellness resorts promising "transformative calm" and "spiritual alignment" [@Sezerel2026]. These offerings can be genuinely valuable to some people, leading to lasting positive habitual change. However, the argument has been made that mindfulness has been stripped of its original value and communal ethics, and has been appropriated by institutions as a tool for  consumption. It *becomes* the product rather than the practice.

The term McMindfulness was coined by Dr. Miles Neale, describing "*a feeding frenzy of spiritual practices that provide immediate nutrition but no long-term sustenance*." You can find the article [online](https://docslib.org/doc/6029907/mcmindfulness-and-frozen-yoga-rediscovering-the-essential-teachings-of-ethics-and-wisdom). It has since been popularized by Purser in his book McMindfulness [@Purser2019]. Apart from having a great title, it critiques industry practices seeking to capitalize on trends, targeting largely wealthy individuals. Retreats starting at £200 per night commodify interiority, framing ethical travel not as a shared social commitment but as a luxury purchase [@Sezerel2026], something you earn like a status symbol, with the additional benefit that it makes you feel *responsible*. Mindfulness in this commercialised form often only serves middle-class and wealthy consumers who can actually afford it, perpetuating social divides while *appearing* to offer an ethical alternative to mass tourism [@Sezerel2026].

Considering our mimetic desire model from Girard, the problem runs deeper than class. "Mindful travel" has itself become a mimetic object. When celebrities and upper-middle-class influencers perform mindfulness (sun salutations at sunrise, handwritten journal pages beside herbal tea, stillness by the ocean) they become models for a new form of aspirational desire. People do not pursue mindful travel because they have reflected carefully on the ethics of consumption. They pursue it because *someone they admire is pursuing it*, and thus, it is deemed desirable. The critique of travel and the solution of "Mindful travel" becomes just another product. The escape becomes another destination. 

## Hashtag Anti-Inspo?

So, am I a fraud if I travel somewhere after being inspired by a photograph? Should every photo I take be an original, un-photographed place? Can I not recreate a photograph I've seen before? Do we start a new #anti-inspo campaign to combat the 31.4 *million* (!) #inspo tags seeking to plant desire for their own validation?

Probably not. Girard was not arguing that imitation is inherently wrong, or that borrowed desires are fraudulent. The problem is not that we are influenced by others. The problem is **unconscious** imitation. I.e., traveling somewhere, photographing something, posting it, without ever asking why, and without noticing that the whole sequence was handed to you by an algorithm.

There are many reasons why an object may be desirable and copied. I have done it. There are photographs I have taken that are the same composition I first saw on Instagram with the same framing, same light, same spot. But I had a conscious reason for it, where I wanted to understand how the image was made. This goes *without* claiming it makes one a more legitimate visitor than anyone else in the queue. The distinction here that matters is not why you copied, it is whether you knew you were copying, and whether you brought anything of your own to it.

The moment you ask "do *I* actually want this, or was I *sold* it?" you are looking outside-in on the mimetic desire loop. You might still go to Santorini, and you might still take the same photograph, and it might still be performative. But you will at least go differently, and in that difference is the only answer this kind of problem admits. The greater question is, then, is this performance of the projected self really worth it?         


## Practical Thoughts

With this in mind, I thought I'd end with some questions. Maybe this isn't so applicable to the general tourist but nevertheless, might offer a means of reflection... 

- **Can I engage economically?** Overtourism is partly a consequence of tourism's economic gains being captured by large platforms and brands rather than local communities. Eating in family-run restaurants, staying in locally owned accommodation, buying directly from makers rather than souvenir shops might not solve the problem at scale, but it shifts the balance in the right direction.

- **Do I need to tag the location?** Not every location needs to be broadcast. Some places, particularly fragile or off-the-beaten-track ones, genuinely benefit from obscurity. If a location is unknown, sharing it publicly is a decision with consequences. Part of the game is finding that hidden beautiful location, so, maybe it's good to keep some mystery.   

- **What is it about the image?** When you find yourself chasing a specific image, one you have seen a dozen times across a dozen accounts, ask what you are actually after. Is it the place, or is it the image of the place? For me, trying to recreate an image is a good exercise in learning why you like the image in the first place. But be honest with it. 
  
- **Is "detourism" possible?** The concept (exploring what is nearby rather than chasing the iconic) has been proposed as one response to the mimetic cycles driving unsustainable tourism [@Cristini2022]. It is also, often, photographically more interesting. The famous view has been taken, make a new one, challenge yourself.

## Thanks for Reading

Individual choices are not nothing. The problems here are structural, and no photographer or influencer solves them alone. But the thing that photography at its best actually teaches, *how* to look, *how* to attend, *how* to be present with a place rather than merely extracting an image from it. This is perhaps the disposition that helps? Not a hashtag campaign. Not a wellness retreat. Just the habit of asking why you are somewhere, and what you actually see when you get there.
